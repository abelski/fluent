# Spaced repetition — what we can claim, and what we cannot

Internal reference for #31. Written because the temptation to put "scientific research proves…"
next to a Buy button is strong and most of the specific numbers people reach for are invented.

Every DOI below was verified against the publisher record on 2026-09-16.

## Claims we can make

| Claim | Source | DOI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spaced study beats massed study. Meta-analysis of 839 assessments across 317 experiments in 184 papers. | Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006), *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3), 354–380 | `10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354` |
| The best gap between reviews scales with how long you want to remember; too short and too long both lose retention. | Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008), *Psychological Science* 19(11), 1095–1102 | `10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x` |
| Being tested on a word beats re-reading it. On foreign-language vocabulary specifically: repeated *study* after first correct recall had no effect on delayed recall; repeated *testing* had a large one. | Karpicke & Roediger (2008), *Science* 319(5865), 966–968 | `10.1126/science.1152408` |
| Re-reading wins at 5 minutes; testing wins at 2 days and 1 week. | Roediger & Karpicke (2006), *Psychological Science* 17(3), 249–255 | `10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x` |
| Personalised review scheduling in a real semester-long middle-school language course produced a **16.5%** gain in end-of-semester retention over normal practice. | Lindsey, Shroyer, Pashler & Mozer (2014), *Psychological Science* 25(3), 639–647 | `10.1177/0956797613504302` |
| A model-computed practice schedule beats a naive one, with large effect sizes on recall and latency. | Pavlik & Anderson (2008), *J. Exp. Psychol.: Applied* 14(2), 101–117 | `10.1037/1076-898X.14.2.101` |

Karpicke & Roediger (2008) is the strongest one for us: the material was foreign-language
vocabulary pairs, which is literally what Fluent teaches. Lindsey (2014) is the strongest for
"this works in a shipped product over months, not in a one-hour lab session".

One more from that paper worth knowing: **students' predictions of their own performance were
uncorrelated with their actual performance.** People genuinely cannot feel which words they are
losing. That is the honest argument for letting the app choose.

## Claims we must NOT make

- **"20% overdue means efficiency drops dramatically."** No study reports this. No study reports any
  overdue-percentage threshold. It was a plausible-sounding number, not a finding.
- **Any specific "X% overdue → Y% worse learning" figure.** The literature gives optimal *gaps*, not
  backlog thresholds.
- **"85–90% retention is the scientifically optimal target."** That range comes from FSRS/Anki
  simulation and community practice, not from a peer-reviewed result. It is a reasonable default;
  it is not a citation.
- **"Research says you must clear your backlog before learning new words."** Nobody tested this.
  What follows from Cepeda (2008) is that a review delayed far past its optimal gap retains less —
  which is an argument for reviewing sooner, not a proven rule about blocking new material.

## The load-bearing distinction

Reviewing before you forget is proven. *Refusing to teach you something new until you have* is a
product decision we are making, and we should say so in our own voice rather than borrow authority
we do not have. The proven part is strong enough on its own.
